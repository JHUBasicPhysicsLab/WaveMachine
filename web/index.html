About the wave machine

Here we simulate the dynamics of the torsional wave machine pictured above. It consists of a set of horizontal rods connected to one another by a wire. As the rods oscillate in a vertical plane, transverse waves propagate through the device. The torsional wave machine illustrates several important physical concepts.

Forbidden frequency band

You might expect that waves travel freely from one end of the machine to the other. It turns out that this expectation works for some waves but not for others. Waves with low frequencies cannot travel through it. If you rock one end of the wave machine periodically and slowly, the other end will remain stationary. The phenomenon of forbidden frequency bands exists in many other physical systems. Electromagnetic waves (e.g. visible light) cannot propagate in metals. The ionosphere reflects back low-frequency radio waves. Our wave machine lets you examine how far a low-frequency wave can propagate into the system before it is “turned back.”

Standing waves

Waves with sufficiently high frequencies are able to propagate through the machine. In this case we have a further complication: our system is finite and waves are reflected back at the edges. The reflections interfere with the original wave and may enhance or suppress the net amplitude. The highest amplitude is reached when the original wave and its reflections are matched at their crests and troughs. In such cases each reflection enhances the net amplitude of the wave and we find a resonance. Since it is hard to keep track of multiple reflections, it is more convenient to think of standing (rather than running) waves. A resonance corresponds to a standing wave that “fits” in a particular way between the edges. You can observe resonances visually. For a more accurate measurement, the second panel shows the energy of the waves as a function of the frequency of the external force.

The model

The simulation has 12 rods. Rod n is turned by an angle θn and obeys

θ̈n = s²(θn+1 − 2θn + θn−1) − ω₀²θn − bθ̇n

The first term is the twist of the wire between neighboring rods. The second is gravity: the wire holds each rod above its center of mass, so a turned rod is pulled back toward horizontal. The third is friction. Here s² = 1 and ω₀ = 0.5 rad/s, so waves with ω < 0.5 rad/s are in the forbidden band. The first rod (red) also feels an external torque τ₀ cos ωt. The drive frequency ω changes slowly at the sweep rate, so the energy plot traces out the response of the machine across a range of frequencies.

How to use it

  • Set the drive frequency and sweep rate with the sliders, or type a value and press Enter. It can help to pause while you do this. A negative sweep rate sweeps downward; zero holds the frequency fixed.
  • Watch for peaks in the energy plot. These are the resonances. Click on the plot to read off the frequency and energy at any point.
  • A fast sweep broadens the peaks, and the first two resonances are close together, so slow the sweep down to separate them. It takes time to build up a resonance, so the peaks lag behind the drive. Sweep in both directions: the true resonance lies between the peak positions.
  • Clamp the right end with the checkbox, or by clicking the last rod, to compare a free end with a fixed one.
  • “Show predicted resonances” marks the normal-mode frequencies of the 12-rod model and shades the forbidden band. Try to measure them yourself before turning it on.
